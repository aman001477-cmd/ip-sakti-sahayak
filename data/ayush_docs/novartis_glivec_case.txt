NOVARTIS v. UNION OF INDIA (2013) — THE GLIVEC CASE
Landmark Patent Case Study | Indian Patents Act, Section 3(d)

1. BACKGROUND
Novartis AG filed a patent application in India (Chennai Patent Office, Application No. 1602/MAS/1998)
for the beta-crystalline form of Imatinib Mesylate, the active ingredient in the anti-cancer drug
Glivec/Gleevec used in treating Chronic Myeloid Leukaemia (CML) and gastrointestinal stromal tumours.

Imatinib in its free-base form was disclosed in an earlier 1993 patent (granted in many countries but
never patented in India, since India did not grant product patents for pharmaceuticals before 2005).
Novartis claimed the beta-crystalline salt form was a new and patentable invention.

2. REJECTION BY THE PATENT OFFICE (2006)
The Assistant Controller of Patents, Chennai, rejected the application. Key grounds:
(a) Section 3(d) — the beta-crystalline form was merely a new form of a known substance (imatinib)
    without demonstrating significantly enhanced therapeutic efficacy;
(b) Lack of novelty and inventive step in view of the earlier disclosed imatinib matter.

3. THE LEGAL BATTLE
- Novartis appealed to the Intellectual Property Appellate Board (IPAB), which in 2009 upheld the
  rejection on Section 3(d) grounds (while holding the invention novel and non-obvious).
- Novartis challenged the constitutional validity of Section 3(d) before the Madras High Court,
  arguing it was vague and TRIPS-incompatible. The High Court upheld Section 3(d).
- Final appeal reached the Supreme Court of India.

4. SUPREME COURT JUDGMENT (1 APRIL 2013)
A two-judge bench (Justices Aftab Alam and Ranjana Desai) dismissed Novartis's appeal:
(a) MEANING OF "EFFICACY" UNDER SECTION 3(d): Efficacy means THERAPEUTIC efficacy, not merely
    improved physical properties such as solubility, stability, flow properties or bioavailability.
    Novartis showed about 30% higher bioavailability but no evidence of enhanced therapeutic effect.
(b) EVERGREENING: The Court held that Section 3(d) was specifically designed to prevent
    "evergreening" — extending patent monopolies through minor modifications of known drugs.
(c) TRIPS COMPATIBILITY: The Court held that Article 27 of TRIPS gives member countries the freedom
    to define the criteria of patentability (novelty, inventive step, industrial application) for
    themselves. Section 3(d) is a permissible domestic standard.
(d) The claimed invention therefore failed the test of Section 3(d) and no patent could be granted.

5. SIGNIFICANCE
- Confirmed India as the global leader in preventing pharmaceutical evergreening.
- Protected access to affordable medicines: generic imatinib (by Natco, Cipla and others) costs a
  small fraction of the patented drug's price.
- Section 3(d) became the most debated provision of Indian patent law worldwide.
- The judgment is routinely cited in patent examinations and oppositions involving pharmaceutical
  derivatives (salts, polymorphs, isomers, combinations).
