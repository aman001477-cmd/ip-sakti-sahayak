BAYER v. NATCO (2012) — INDIA'S FIRST COMPULSORY LICENCE
Landmark Patent Case Study | Indian Patents Act, Section 84

1. BACKGROUND
Bayer Corporation held Indian Patent No. 215758 for Sorafenib Tosylate, marketed as Nexavar, a drug
used in the treatment of advanced kidney cancer and liver cancer. The patented drug was sold in India
at approximately Rs. 2,80,000 per month of treatment, imported rather than manufactured in India.

Natco Pharma, an Indian generic manufacturer, applied to the Controller General of Patents for a
compulsory licence under Section 84 of the Patents Act, 1970, to manufacture and sell a generic
version at about Rs. 8,800 per month.

2. GROUNDS UNDER SECTION 84
Section 84 permits any interested person to seek a compulsory licence after three years from grant
where:
(a) the reasonable requirements of the public with respect to the patented invention have not been
    satisfied; OR
(b) the patented invention is not available to the public at a reasonably affordable price; OR
(c) the patented invention is not worked in the territory of India.

3. CONTROLLER'S DECISION (9 MARCH 2012)
The Controller granted India's first post-TRIPS compulsory licence to Natco, holding that all three
conditions were satisfied:
(a) PUBLIC REQUIREMENTS NOT MET: Only a tiny fraction of eligible patients could access Nexavar;
    Bayer's own data showed negligible sales volumes against the estimated patient population.
(b) NOT AFFORDABLY PRICED: Rs. 2.8 lakh per month was beyond the reach of the public; Natco's
    Rs. 8,800 price made treatment accessible.
(c) NOT WORKED IN INDIA: Bayer only imported the drug; mere importation, without local
    manufacturing supported by reasons, did not amount to "working" in India.
Royalty was fixed at 6% of net sales (later enhanced to 7% by the IPAB in 2013).

4. APPEALS AND OUTCOME
- The IPAB (2013) upheld the licence with enhanced 7% royalty.
- The Bombay High Court (2014) upheld the grant, confirming that "worked in India" generally means
  local manufacturing and that import alone is insufficient without justification.
- The licence ran its course and demonstrated that the compulsory-licensing mechanism is workable.

5. SIGNIFICANCE
- Established the practical interpretation of Section 84's three grounds.
- Clarified that "working" a patent in India ordinarily requires local manufacture, not mere import.
- Balanced patentee rights with public health; widely studied as a model TRIPS-flexibility use.
- Companion litigation (Bayer v. Cipla infringement suit over the same drug) clarified the line
  between lawful compulsory licensing and infringement.
