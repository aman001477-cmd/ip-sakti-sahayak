COMPULSORY LICENSING BEYOND SECTION 84 — SECTIONS 92, 92A, 100–103
Indian Patents Act, 1970 | Doha Declaration and TRIPS Flexibilities

(For Section 84 and the Bayer-Natco Nexavar licence, see bayer_natco_cl_case.txt.)

1. SECTION 92 — NATIONAL EMERGENCY / PUBLIC NON-COMMERCIAL USE
Where a national emergency, extreme urgency or public non-commercial use is notified by the
Central Government, the Controller may grant licences for the relevant patents without the
Section 84 three-year wait. Never used so far in India, but the provision gained global attention
during the COVID-19 vaccine-access debate.

2. SECTION 92A — EXPORT LICENCES (DOHA PARAGRAPH 6 SYSTEM)
Inserted to implement the 2003 WTO Decision/Doha Declaration mechanism: manufacture and export
of patented pharmaceuticals to countries with insufficient manufacturing capacity, on notified
terms with adequate remuneration and anti-diversion safeguards.

3. GOVERNMENT USE — SECTIONS 100–103
The Government (or persons authorised by it) may use any patented invention for Government
purposes — including mere import — with compensation to the patentee settled by agreement or by
the High Court. Distinct from compulsory licensing: no three-year wait, no Section 84 procedure.

4. DOHA DECLARATION ON TRIPS AND PUBLIC HEALTH (2001)
WTO members affirmed that TRIPS "does not and should not prevent members from taking measures
to protect public health", expressly preserving rights to grant compulsory licences, determine
their grounds, and define national emergencies. The 2017 TRIPS Amendment (Article 31bis)
permanently embedded the export mechanism.

5. RELATED SAFEGUARDS IN INDIAN LAW
- Section 107A (Bolar provision): making/using a patented invention for regulatory-approval
  research and development is NOT infringement — enables generics to be approval-ready at
  patent expiry.
- Parallel importation (Section 107A(b)): import of legitimately-marketed patented products.
- Pre-grant opposition (Section 25(1)) and Section 3(d)/(p) bars as complementary access tools.
